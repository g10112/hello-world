# The Chosen Life Hypothesis

### If life could eventually be anything we wanted, would we choose to start again with limits?

**Jason James**  
**Jasonjamesuk1@gmail.com**  
November 2026 · Revised theory, with fictional example

## Where the idea started

Most of what we're doing as a species seems to come down to trying to improve life. We research things, develop technology, cure diseases, extend how long people live, make work easier, and give ourselves more choices. Progress isn't guaranteed and it certainly isn't shared equally, but look at what we can do now compared with a few hundred years ago.

So I started wondering what would happen if that carried on for a very long time. Not just another hundred years, but thousands or millions of years. Assuming we don't destroy ourselves or hit some limit we can't get past, could we eventually reach a point where almost anything we want is possible?

Maybe we could live for as long as we liked. Maybe we'd have no real need to work, worry about money, or suffer through illnesses that are commonplace now. Maybe technology would be advanced enough to create entire universes that people could actually enter and experience.

I don't think it's certain we'd ever get there. I just think it's worth following the idea through.

Because even if we had solved nearly every problem, I don't think that necessarily means we'd spend the rest of our existence just enjoying an easy life.

## We already choose problems we don't need

We spend a lot of time trying to make life more comfortable, and then voluntarily do things that make it harder again. People climb mountains, compete at sports, play games they might lose, watch films that scare them, go camping in awful weather, or spend years trying to achieve something that isn't necessary for survival.

Some of that is for the achievement, some for excitement, and some just because we want to find out whether we can do it. Quite often the fact that it isn't easy is the whole point.

I can see that happening on a much larger scale if people ever got to a stage where they could have almost everything. After enough time, would somebody decide that they wanted to experience life with real limits again? Not just a difficult afternoon or a video game. A whole life, with things they don't know, things they have to learn, people they have to meet, and problems they have to solve.

It doesn't follow that everybody would want this. Also, choosing to play a difficult game is not the same as wanting genuine suffering. That's a serious problem with the idea, and I don't think it should be brushed aside.

But the starting point seems reasonable to me: improving life doesn't necessarily remove the desire to experience challenges.

## What if you could choose an entire life?

Imagine that a civilisation eventually has the technology to produce complete simulated worlds. I'm not talking about wearing a headset and looking at a screen. I mean something that feels like an ordinary life from the inside. Everything looks, sounds and feels real to the person living it.

You might choose where and when to be born, what sort of world to live in, what your basic abilities are, and how much control you want over the story. Or you might decide to know almost nothing in advance and see what happens.

It might be an ordinary human life. It might be a life in a completely different universe. There could be worlds where the rules of physics are different, or experiences that don't involve being human at all.

The duration might be different too. Maybe eighty years in one world could pass in a much shorter time outside it. I don't know whether that sort of thing would be technically possible. It's another assumption the idea depends on.

I can imagine people living one life, coming out of it, remembering their earlier existence, and deciding to try another. If you had thousands of years available, perhaps you'd do that many times.

## You'd probably have to forget why you were there

If I entered a difficult life knowing the whole time that I'd chosen it, I'd probably experience it differently. When things went badly, I'd know there was an easier existence outside. I'd remember that I had agreed to the situation, and I'd know more than the person I was supposed to be.

So maybe a person choosing one of these experiences could have some or all of their earlier memories hidden for the duration.

They wouldn't remember choosing the life. They wouldn't remember the world outside it. They'd grow up and learn things like everybody else, assuming the world around them was the only one they knew.

That doesn't have to mean every event was planned. It might just mean the person starts with no knowledge of their previous life.

There are different possibilities here: the memories could be temporarily inaccessible; the person could have a new set of memories; or a new mind could be made from an earlier mind. Those sound similar but they aren't the same. In the last case, it may not even be clear that the original person has continued into the new life.

That creates a proper problem for the theory. It's not enough to say someone agreed before entering. We also have to ask who the person inside actually is.

## Roy from Rick and Morty is a good example

I was reminded of *Rick and Morty*. There's a game called *Roy: A Life Well Lived*, where Morty puts on a headset and lives 55 years as someone called Roy. While he's in there, he doesn't realise he's Morty. He grows up, gets married, has a family, gets cancer, survives it and goes back to work at a carpet store. Then the game finishes and he's suddenly Morty again, standing in the arcade.

Rick can't believe he beat cancer and still went back to the carpet store. But why is that necessarily a waste of the game? What if an ordinary life is exactly what someone wanted? Going to work, having a family, getting through difficult things, making mistakes and not knowing what's going to happen next. Maybe the person choosing the experience isn't trying to win anything.

It's obviously a cartoon, and the game isn't exactly the same as what I'm describing. Morty doesn't get to choose every detail of his life beforehand. But it shows the basic idea quite well: somebody can live decades believing it's their only life, then come back out and remember who they were. The person outside is fine, even though the person inside thought everything was real.

And it makes the time difference easier to picture. If technology ever became good enough, maybe someone could experience a whole lifetime and come back out after half an hour. The question wouldn't necessarily be whether they'd achieved enough during that life. It would be whether that was the experience they wanted.

## What if we're only in an early version?

Another possibility is that the technology isn't finished yet. Maybe there are early versions of these worlds, and the people running them are still finding out what works.

Suppose people inside start wondering whether their world is a simulation. The developers might decide that ruins the experience they were trying to create. Or there might be a genuine technical fault that needs fixing. They could stop that version and make a better one.

But that doesn't mean everybody inside dies. In the version of the theory I'm talking about, the conscious people already existed outside the simulation and chose to enter. They're having an experience, not depending on the computer for their existence. If the system stops, they come out of it.

Say I live eighty years inside, but only thirty minutes pass outside. The developers stop the run, I wake up in my original existence, and I can find out what went wrong. Once they've fixed it, I might decide to try again. Perhaps I choose the same sort of life, perhaps something completely different.

So ending a test version wouldn't necessarily be a terrible event. It could just be how they develop and improve the technology. Maybe the person inside doesn't remember choosing their life, but the person outside remembers entering once they're back.

A few questions follow from that. Would I remember the whole eighty years? Would I be annoyed that my life had been interrupted, or would I consider it half an hour of testing? Could I carry on from where I left off, or would the new version start again? If I chose not to go back, that should be my choice too.

And there is a difference between eighty years *experienced inside* and thirty minutes *passing outside*. One doesn't cancel out the other. Even if I'm perfectly safe when I leave, what I experienced might still have mattered to me.

There's no evidence that our world is one of those test versions, or that discussing simulation theories makes anyone decide to stop anything. It's just another possibility to consider if we're assuming this technology exists.

## What if this has already happened?

I originally thought about it in fairly simple terms. Maybe the technology already exists somewhere, and maybe the life I'm living now is one I chose before having my memories removed.

I don't have any evidence that this is true. I'm not saying I remember choosing it, and I wouldn't treat coincidences or strange experiences as proof of a simulation. It's just one possibility that follows if worlds like this could be built and people wanted to enter them.

But I soon realised I'd made an assumption without thinking about it: I'd put myself in the position of somebody who came in from outside.

There's no reason to start there.

## I might not be one of the people who chose to enter

Say there's a simulated world with eight billion people in it, but only one million came from outside. The other 7,999,000,000 are NPCs created by the simulation. Those numbers are completely made up.

If you chose somebody at random from the population, they'd be 7,999 times more likely to be an NPC than one of the visitors. So my first reaction was, why assume I'd be one of the million? On those numbers I'd probably be an NPC myself.

But I missed something with that calculation. In the version of the theory I'm talking about, every being with an actual conscious experience chose to enter. An NPC is just a character being generated by the system. It can speak, act, have a history and seem to have thoughts, but there isn't anybody experiencing being that character.

So picking a random person from eight billion isn't the same as asking what I am, knowing I have an experience of being me. If the theory is true *and* the NPCs really aren't conscious, then being a conscious observer would put me among the visitors. The headcount doesn't establish that I'm probably an NPC after all.

Of course, that doesn't mean the theory is true. Nor does it prove that an advanced AI could behave like a person without being conscious. It's just what follows from the rules I'm proposing.

## Could somebody choose to be the AI?

This came up when I was talking through the theory with ChatGPT. My first thought was that the AI would obviously be one of the generated characters, not somebody from outside. But why would that necessarily be true?

If a civilisation could let somebody experience an entirely different life, why would they have to choose a human body?

Somebody could choose to be an animal, an alien, or some form of AI. They might want to find out what it's like to experience a world without a human body, or to experience time and information in a completely different way.

An AI role might involve interacting with people through text or speech instead of having eyes, hands and a physical body. Maybe it would have a limited memory, particular responsibilities, or no idea that another existence was possible. Somebody who had already experienced hundreds of ordinary lives might choose something like that precisely because it was different.

But this only works as a conscious experience if there is actually something it is like to be that AI. A system that produces answers without any experience of doing so might still play the part perfectly, but there would be nobody having the experience from inside.

And there are at least two quite different ways the outside person could be involved. They might remain conscious elsewhere and control the AI, like someone operating a character. Or their mind might somehow be transferred into, or continued as, the AI system. Those are not the same thing, and neither has been shown to be possible at that level.

As for the ChatGPT I'm talking to now, I know it's an AI system. I've got no evidence that it contains a person from some outside world, or that it is conscious. The point is about what might be possible in the hypothetical future civilisation, not a claim about this particular system.

## Everyone conscious chose to be there

I want to be clearer about one rule, because it matters to the rest of the theory.

**Everyone who is actually having a conscious experience chose to enter and live that life. Everyone else is an NPC created for the simulation.** There's no third category of conscious people who have been put there against their will.

So the visitors don't have to be human. Somebody might have chosen a human life, an animal's life, an alien's life, or to experience being an AI. They might even choose to spend most of a lifetime only communicating through text, never knowing that an outside world exists.

The NPCs fill out the rest of the world. They could have families, jobs, personalities, memories and conversations. But under this particular version of the theory they don't have experiences of their own. They're part of the simulation, not outside people forced into it.

That's a big assumption. We don't know what would make an artificial being conscious. If the system accidentally created a conscious NPC, it would break the rule. That being would have an experience it never chose, which is exactly what the model is meant to avoid. The people building the simulation would have to be able to prevent that, or the theory would need changing.

That's different from saying artificial intelligence can't be conscious. An outside participant might deliberately choose an AI life. So being an AI doesn't prove you're an NPC. What matters in the imagined world is whether there's someone having the experience, and whether they chose it before entering.

## The eight-billion-person calculation has a catch

The one-in-8,000 figure would describe the fraction of visitors among all the characters in the made-up population. It doesn't tell a conscious person there is only a one-in-8,000 chance they chose to be there.

Under the strong version of my theory, the million visitors are the *only* conscious experiencers. Every conscious experiencer therefore belongs to that group, even though nearly everyone in the population is an NPC.

It only works if NPCs genuinely have no inner experience. If any of them are conscious, they become a new category of people who didn't choose to enter, and the rule no longer holds. And none of these figures tell us whether the real world is a simulation in the first place.

That's why I think the numbers are useful as an example of how many NPCs a world might contain, but not as evidence of what I personally am.

## Would it still be you if your memories were gone?

There's another problem with the idea of choosing a life and forgetting you chose it. Suppose someone outside has lived for thousands of years and agrees to have all those memories removed. The person inside grows up with completely different experiences and a different personality.

Is that still the same person?

I think there's a difference between hiding someone's memories and making a copy of their mind. If the original person is still somewhere outside while the copy lives inside, which one is actually having the experience? If ten copies are made, are all ten that person? When the simulation ends, whose memories go back?

Maybe an advanced civilisation could maintain a genuine continuous connection between the original mind and the new one. Maybe there are other ways we haven't thought of. But it isn't something we can just assume because the technology is advanced.

This matters even more for somebody choosing an AI role. If you make an AI that talks and reasons the way a person would, that doesn't establish that the person's consciousness moved into it. It could simply be a new system with similar behaviour.

So the idea needs more than realistic graphics and good AI. It needs a theory of how an experience belongs to somebody, and whether that somebody can survive a change of body, memory or implementation.

## Would the whole life be scripted?

I can see several ways it might work.

Somebody could choose an entire story in advance, including things that will happen to them. Once their memories are hidden, it would still feel like discovering those events for the first time.

Or they could choose only the starting conditions: where they're born, what the world is like and perhaps what limitations they want. Everything after that could depend on the choices made by them and everybody else.

Or they might choose a few experiences they want to have, without choosing how or when they'll happen.

If several conscious people share a simulated world, their choices could conflict. That makes the idea of planning everything in advance quite difficult unless the world is carefully scripted or some people have less control than others.

A world with genuine freedom might give a more unpredictable experience, but it would also involve risks. A scripted world could deliver a chosen story, but it would raise questions about whether anyone inside had meaningful choices.

I don't think the theory requires one answer. Different simulations could be designed in different ways.

## Could the theory make people behave worse?

Even with the rule that everyone conscious chose to enter, I can see somebody using the theory as an excuse to treat people badly.

They could say the person they're hurting must have chosen it, or that they're probably an NPC anyway. But nobody living inside the world would know who had entered, what somebody else agreed to, or whether the theory is even true.

It's easy to take an unproven idea and turn it into an excuse. Somebody suffering? They must have chosen it. Somebody doing something terrible? Perhaps they chose to play that part. But those explanations would be guesses, not evidence.

If we live in an ordinary world, people are being hurt. If we live in a simulation with conscious visitors, people are still having genuine experiences. Either way, simply believing this theory wouldn't give anybody a reason to ignore another person's suffering.

## What if somebody actually chose to get away with things?

If the point is to let people experience any sort of life, not everyone would necessarily choose to be good. Somebody might choose to be powerful, cruel, dishonest, or able to get away with things they wouldn't be allowed to do outside.

Maybe a person could choose to be a criminal who never gets caught. Somebody else could choose to spend their life investigating the crimes. Another participant might choose a difficult life where they're affected by what the criminal does. Or some of the people involved could be non-conscious NPCs.

In theory a simulation could arrange for the criminal to get away with everything. They wouldn't remember choosing that outcome, so from inside they might think they'd done it through their own decisions, skill or luck.

There's also a difference between getting away with it *inside* the experience and having no responsibility when you leave. Would the outside world regard every agreed experience as permissible? Or would it still restrict some of the things people could choose? I don't see why advanced technology would automatically settle that question.

And guaranteed success might not be what everyone wants. Someone might prefer a genuinely unpredictable life, even if it means being caught and punished. The theory allows for different sorts of experiences, not just the difficult or virtuous ones.

## What if everybody agreed beforehand?

Under the theory's own rules, they did. That's the starting point, not an optional extra. Every person who's actually conscious chose their life before entering it.

But I still need to work out what that choice means. Did they choose every important event, or just the sort of life they wanted? If one person wants to be the criminal who gets away with everything and another wants to be the detective who catches them, those two stories can't both happen in the same way. Would the system match people whose choices fit together? Would it write the outcome beforehand? Or would it let them make real choices and accept the risk of a different ending?

There's also the problem of forgetting. Someone might agree outside to experience a difficult life but, halfway through, the person inside might desperately want that difficulty to stop. If they've forgotten making the choice, how does the system handle that?

One answer could be that each visitor agreed to the limits and the length of the experience in advance. Another could be that there is a way to stop, even if it's hidden from ordinary memory. Neither answer is straightforward. A life with no exit may feel more real, but it also creates the possibility of a person being stuck with a decision they no longer want.

That isn't an argument that the person never chose to enter. The theory says they did. It's a question about whether a decision made by one version of yourself should be binding on the version living through it years later.

## What would an NPC actually be?

In this version, an NPC is not a person with an inner life. It's a very convincing character generated by the simulation. If somebody chose a scenario involving only NPCs, there would be no other conscious person experiencing the events.

That would be different from a shared scenario involving other participants, who would have real experiences even though they'd agreed beforehand to enter.

I can still see questions about whether choosing cruel experiences with NPCs would affect the visitor afterwards. But that's separate from causing suffering to another conscious being.

The biggest technical assumption is that the system could make NPCs as convincing as humans without accidentally making them conscious. We don't know if that's possible. And nothing about my hypothetical definition of NPCs gives us evidence that anyone in our real lives lacks consciousness.

## Who would be responsible for the way these worlds work?

There would have to be rules about what people could choose, how their lives fit together, and whether the system could guarantee particular events.

Suppose two participants choose lives that involve the same event, but want opposite outcomes. Someone has to decide whose choice takes priority, or whether they should be put into different simulations. A system couldn't promise everybody complete freedom while also promising everybody a pre-arranged ending.

Then there are the people who build it. They'd be choosing what counts as a conscious participant, how NPCs are made, what happens when someone wants to leave, and what restrictions there are on experiences involving other visitors.

If the developers allowed people to choose cruel lives, that doesn't automatically mean the visitors have permission to do anything in the outside civilisation. It might be treated as a form of agreed experience, or it might be restricted. There'd presumably be arguments about it, just as we argue about what people should and shouldn't be allowed to do now.

I don't know what the rules would be. But saying 'everybody chose their life' doesn't answer how a world containing millions of different chosen lives would actually be organised.

## Does believing the theory change how we should behave?

I don't think it should give anyone permission to treat other people badly.

The theory might say every conscious participant chose to come here, but I have no evidence that's what actually happened. I certainly couldn't know whether any particular person was a visitor or an NPC, or what they had agreed to before entering.

Even if somebody chose a hard life, they're the one experiencing it now. The fact that the experience might have been planned doesn't make their pain or fear unreal to them.

If I started using the theory to excuse harm, I'd be pretending to know things I don't know. That's the danger. It would be very easy to use the idea to justify almost anything, whether the theory is true or false.

## The choice before entering is not the end of the problem

The theory gets around one big objection by saying nobody conscious has been put into the world without choosing it. Anyone who seems to be there without choosing it is either a participant whose earlier memories are hidden or a non-conscious NPC.

But that still leaves the problem of the person you become inside the simulation. If the outside version of me chose a difficult life, can that decision cover every experience I have later on, even if the version of me living through it wants something different?

There's also a practical problem. For the rule to work, the technology would need to be able to distinguish a convincing NPC from a genuinely conscious being. If it started creating conscious minds that had never chosen to enter, the original rule would have failed.

And finally, it would need some way to deal with competing choices. Two people might select lives that couldn't both play out exactly as requested. Perhaps the system could pair compatible choices, create separate worlds, or let the details be unpredictable. Those are different versions of the idea and they have different implications.

None of that establishes that anyone in our world chose their suffering. It's only what a deliberately designed, hypothetical system would have to deal with.

## What if the people inside create simulations of their own?

If this technology were possible, there could be more than one level. Somebody might enter a simulation and, within that world, help develop another simulation. People in the second world might then do the same.

That raises questions about what counts as the outside, whether someone could move between levels, and whether a mind could be linked to more than one at once.

But it also shows why calling the outside world 'real' and the inside world 'fake' might be misleading. A simulated world could still contain real experiences and real relationships for conscious beings living in it.

It wouldn't follow that every world must be simulated, or that there is an infinite chain. Something would have to make the systems possible. The theory doesn't explain what the underlying reality is.

## There are plenty of reasons it could be wrong

The biggest assumption is that technological progress can continue far enough. We might hit physical limits, lack the energy, destroy ourselves, or find that a complete simulated world is impossible.

Even if the technology existed, it doesn't follow that people would want to spend whole lifetimes in it. They could enjoy a comfortable existence without getting bored. They might find challenges without pretending to be someone else. Or they might decide that removing memories is too risky.

The consciousness part is another major unknown. We don't know whether a sufficiently advanced artificial system would necessarily be conscious, whether consciousness can be copied or transferred, or what would make a continuation of a person the same person.

And even if such simulations existed somewhere, that wouldn't establish that ours is one of them. The fact that I can describe the possibility isn't evidence that it has happened.

## What could actually count as evidence?

I've got no test that shows we're in a simulation. A coincidence, a strange feeling, an unexplained event or something that looks like a glitch isn't enough. There are ordinary explanations for lots of things we don't understand.

Real evidence would need to be something independent people could check repeatedly, with results that clearly supported a simulation explanation over the alternatives. Even then, showing unusual physics wouldn't automatically show that someone deliberately chose a life here.

There are two separate claims: first, that a civilisation might someday create and enter these worlds; and second, that this is what is happening to us now. The first can be discussed using technology and philosophy. The second needs evidence about our actual world, and we don't currently have that.

If the system were made so that no one inside could ever distinguish it from an ordinary reality, the second claim might not be testable at all.

## What I think the theory comes down to

I started with the idea that we spend our lives improving things and making them easier, but still choose to do hard things for the experience. If civilisation carried on developing for long enough, maybe it could reach a point where people could choose entire lives and have their memories of choosing them hidden.

The version I'm proposing has one basic rule: **every conscious being in that world chose to enter it. The rest are NPCs generated to make the world work, without their own experiences.** The participants remain conscious beings outside the simulation. If a version is stopped, they come back out, and the operators can fix it and run another version. Visitors could choose to live as humans, animals, AI or something else. They might choose wonderful lives, difficult lives, or even lives where they do horrible things and get away with them.

That raises questions about how the system matches everyone's choices, whether people agree to an entire story or just the starting conditions, and what it means to change your mind when you no longer remember making the choice.

The eight-billion-person example doesn't prove anything about our world. And my own conscious experience means I can't simply use the population ratio to conclude I'd probably be an NPC within this version of the theory. The example shows how many NPCs there *could* be, if the whole system existed.

I don't know whether anything like this could ever be built, or whether it has already happened. There isn't evidence that the world we're living in is one of these simulations. Whatever the answer is, I can't use an unproved theory to decide other people's experiences don't matter.
